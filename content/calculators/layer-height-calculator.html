The honest summary

If your printer was made in roughly the last six years, this almost certainly does not affect your prints. Prusa's own forum puts it plainly: the concept "was largely for early 3D printers with old design Stepper Drivers and it's not a big issue with Prusa Printers." The same reasoning applies to Bambu, Creality's current range, and any machine running TMC2208 or TMC2209 drivers — which is nearly all of them.

That said, choosing 0.16 mm over 0.15 mm costs you nothing at all. So the practical advice is: don't reorganise your profiles around this, don't expect a visible improvement, but if you are picking a number anyway, you may as well pick one that lands on a full step. This page exists to tell you which ones do and to be straight about the size of the effect.

The formula

full step (mm) = travel per revolution ÷ (motor steps per revolution × gear reduction)
full-step layer heights = full step × 1, 2, 3, …
repeat period = smallest q where (layer height ÷ full step) × q is a whole number

Worked example

Ender 3: 8 mm lead screw, 1.8° motor (200 steps), direct drive.

  • Full step: 8 ÷ 200 = 0.04 mm
  • On full steps: 0.04, 0.08, 0.12, 0.16, 0.20, 0.24, 0.28, 0.32
  • Not: 0.10 (2.5 steps, repeats every 2 layers), 0.15 (3.75 steps, repeats every 4), 0.25 (6.25 steps, repeats every 4)

That last column is the part other calculators don't give you. If you print at 0.15 mm and you can see faint banding, this tells you the pattern from this cause would repeat every 4 layers — 0.6 mm. If the banding you're looking at has a different period, it isn't this; look at your lead screw, Z wobble or extrusion instead.

What the mechanism actually is

A stepper motor has 200 or 400 positions per turn where the rotor is held by the magnet poles. Between those, the driver interpolates by feeding the coils fractional currents. Microstep positions are held with less torque and less positional accuracy than full steps.

Prusa's own description points at a more specific version of this: the Z axis is not always energised, and "if the axis is disabled during micro-step, [the] axis jumps to the closest full step and introduce[s] error." That is a real effect with a real magnitude — up to half a full step, or 20 µm on a 0.04 mm machine — but it happens when motors are de-energised, not continuously through a print.

During printing the effect is subtler: if the layer height is a whole number of full steps, every layer sits at the same point in the motor's electrical cycle, so whatever small positioning error exists is identical on every layer and invisible. If it isn't, layers cycle through a few different positions and any error becomes periodic, which the eye picks up as banding. Consistency is the actual benefit, not accuracy.

The arguments against, and how much they hold

"Homing lands on an arbitrary microstep anyway"

True, and frequently offered as a knockout. It isn't quite. A full-step layer height gives consistent spacing, so every layer sits at the same phase in the step cycle regardless of what offset homing established. The starting point is irrelevant; the repeatability is the point. This objection is weaker than it sounds — but the thing being made consistent is very small to begin with.

"Bed mesh compensation moves Z in microsteps constantly"

This one holds. With mesh compensation active, Z is continuously adjusted as the toolhead moves across the bed, so a layer isn't at a single Z at all and "landing on a full step" stops being meaningful. Most firmware fades compensation out over the first few millimetres, so the objection fully applies below the fade height and stops applying above it — but within that region it's decisive.

"Modern drivers interpolate"

This one also holds, and it's the main reason the idea has aged out. TMC drivers interpolate to 256 microsteps regardless of the configured microstepping, which smooths the current waveform and makes intermediate positions far more stable than they were on an A4988. Combined with a loaded lead screw — which is held mechanically by friction and the weight of the gantry — the residual error is well below what shows on a printed surface.

"The error is too small to see"

Broadly true on modern hardware. Layer-height variation from Z wobble, an eccentric lead screw, inconsistent extrusion or a warped bed is typically much larger than anything microstepping contributes. If you have visible banding, those are the things to check first, and the repeat period above will help you rule this cause in or out.

When it does still apply

  • Older budget printers with A4988 or DRV8825 drivers and no interpolation — the hardware the idea was formed around.
  • Boards with interpolation deliberately disabled, which some people run for specific reasons.
  • Any printer where Z is de-energised between moves, per Prusa's disabled-axis note.

Conversely it is essentially irrelevant on a fine-lead Z (a T8x2 screw has a 0.01 mm full step, so almost any sensible layer height is within a fraction of a step), on belted Z with reduction, and on anything with mesh compensation active for the full print height.

How this differs from other layer height calculators

Prusa publishes one as part of their RepRap calculator, and several other sites list the multiples. Those give you the same arithmetic this page does — full step size and its multiples — and stop there. What's added here:

  • The repeat period for heights that aren't on full steps, so you can match a banding pattern you can actually see against a predicted period rather than guessing.
  • A verdict conditioned on your hardware — driver type and mesh settings — instead of implying the answer is the same for every printer.
  • An explicit statement of how small the effect is, including Prusa's own position that it isn't a big issue on modern machines.

If all you want is the list of multiples, any of those calculators will do and Prusa's is as good as this one.

Choosing a layer height for reasons that matter more

Layer height trades detail against time roughly linearly: 0.12 mm takes about 1.7× as long as 0.20 mm. The nozzle sets the sensible bounds — below about 25% of nozzle diameter the melt is squeezed thin enough that flow gets erratic, and above about 75% the bead can't be pressed properly onto the layer below, so adhesion suffers. Within that range, larger layers bond better and smaller layers look better. Those effects are far larger than anything on this page.
