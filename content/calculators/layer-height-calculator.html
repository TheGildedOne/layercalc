Choosing a layer height on the things that actually change the print

Most layer height calculators list the multiples of your motor's full step and stop. That is one input, and on a modern printer it is close to the least important one. The four that genuinely change the result are above the fold:

  • How long it takes — and whether your hotend's flow limit changes that trade.
  • How visible the stepping is on the sloped and curved surfaces in your model.
  • Whether critical heights land on a layer boundary.
  • Whether it is a full-step multiple — which usually doesn't matter, and the page says when it does.

Stair-stepping: the number that decides how it looks

Layer height alone doesn't tell you whether a surface will look stepped. The slope does. On a surface at angle θ measured from horizontal:

deviation from the true surface (cusp height) = layer height × cos(θ)
horizontal width of each step = layer height ÷ tan(θ)

A vertical wall (θ = 90°) has zero deviation no matter how coarse the layers — which is why a plain box looks fine at 0.3 mm. A 45° slope at 0.2 mm deviates 141 µm and steps 0.2 mm across. A 15° slope at the same 0.2 mm still deviates 193 µm but each step is now 0.75 mm wide, and that width is what makes shallow domes and gentle curves look terraced while steep surfaces look smooth.

This is why "use 0.1 mm for detail" is bad advice in the abstract. If your model is mostly vertical walls, fine layers buy you nothing but time. If it has a shallow dome on top, they buy you a lot.

Worked example

A part with a 30° slope, printed at 0.2 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle.

  • Deviation: 0.2 × cos(30°) = 173 µm — clearly visible
  • Step width: 0.2 ÷ tan(30°) = 0.35 mm
  • To get deviation under 50 µm: 0.05 ÷ cos(30°) = 0.058 mm or finer

That is a big jump — roughly 3.5× the print time. The usual answer is not to print the whole part that fine but to use variable layer height, letting the slicer thin the layers only where the slope demands it. The figure above tells you what to set the minimum to.

Print time, and why it isn't simply 1/height

Time is set by volumetric throughput, not by layer count alone:

throughput (mm³/s) = bead cross-section × speed
bead cross-section = line width × layer height − layer height² × (1 − π/4)

If your hotend's flow limit was already capping speed at the coarser height, then dropping to a finer layer lets you run faster per layer, and the time penalty is smaller than the layer-count ratio suggests. If you were nowhere near the limit, it is close to proportional. The table applies your actual flow figure, and marks with an asterisk any row where the hotend, not your speed setting, is the constraint.

The cross-section formula is the one the Slic3r-derived slicers use — a rectangle with semicircular ends. Plain width × height overstates throughput by 5–10% at typical settings, which is enough to matter when you are near the ceiling. The volumetric speed calculator goes into this in more depth.

One thing the table deliberately doesn't model: per-layer overhead. Travel moves, layer changes and minimum-layer-time cooling all scale with layer count, so very fine layers are somewhat worse in reality than the throughput maths alone suggests.

Feature fit: why a 5 mm boss comes out 4.8 mm

A printer can only stop on a layer boundary. If a dimension that matters isn't a whole number of layers, it lands short or long by up to half a layer:

layers = feature height ÷ layer height
printed height = round(layers) × layer height

5 mm at 0.3 mm is 16.67 layers, so you get 16 (4.8 mm) or 17 (5.1 mm) — up to 200 µm out on a dimension you specified precisely. At 0.2 mm it is exactly 25 layers and comes out right. This bites hardest on parts that mate with hardware: bearing shoulders, standoffs, anything with a specified stack height.

The tool checks your chosen height against a dimension you give it, and lists which heights in the usable range divide it exactly. Note that this is separate from shrinkage, which shifts every dimension by a percentage; this is a quantisation error and no amount of scaling fixes it.

Magic numbers, weighted honestly

A layer height that is an exact multiple of one full motor step means every layer sits at the same point in the motor's electrical cycle, so whatever small positioning error exists is identical on every layer and therefore invisible. If it isn't a multiple, layers cycle through a few positions and the error becomes periodic — which the tool reports as a repeat period, so you can check a banding pattern you can actually see against a predicted one.

How much this matters depends on hardware, and on most current printers the answer is "not at all". Prusa's own forum describes the concept as "largely for early 3D printers with old design Stepper Drivers" and "not a big issue with Prusa Printers". TMC drivers interpolate to 256 microsteps; a loaded lead screw is held mechanically; and where bed mesh compensation is active, Z is being adjusted continuously in microsteps anyway, so the idea of a layer "landing on a full step" stops meaning anything.

It still applies on older A4988 and DRV8825 boards without interpolation, and Prusa notes a specific mechanism worth knowing: a disabled Z axis snaps to the nearest full step, which is an error of up to half a step. The verdict box takes your driver and mesh settings into account rather than giving everyone the same answer.

And the practical note that survives all of it: choosing 0.16 over 0.15 costs nothing. Don't reorganise your profiles around this, but if you're picking a number anyway, you may as well pick one that lands on a step.
