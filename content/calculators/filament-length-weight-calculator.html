The formulas

grams per meter = π × (diameter ÷ 2)² × density (diameter in cm → g/m with ×100)
weight (g) = length (m) × grams per meter
volume (cm³) = weight (g) ÷ density (g/cm³)

Filament is a cylinder, so its volume is cross-section times length, and its weight is volume times density. The cross-section of 1.75 mm filament is 2.405 mm² (0.02405 cm²); of 2.85 mm, 6.379 mm². That 2.65× ratio is why 2.85 mm spools look so short.

Worked example

A print needs 42 m of 1.75 mm PETG. How much does it weigh and how much space does it take?

  • Weight: 42 × 3.05 = 128 g
  • Volume: 128 ÷ 1.27 = 101 cm³
  • At $24/kg the material is 0.128 × 24 = $3.07

How precise is this really?

The arithmetic is exact. The inputs are not, and one of them matters more than people expect: weight scales with the square of the diameter, so a small diameter error becomes a larger weight error.

weight error = (measured ± tolerance)² ÷ nominal² − 1

Good filament holds ±0.02 mm; budget filament ±0.05 mm. On 1.75 mm, ±0.02 mm is about ±2.3% in weight, and ±0.05 mm is nearly ±6%. That is larger than the difference between many of the densities in the table below, which is why the calculator shows the figure alongside the answer.

If it matters, measure. Take calliper readings at several points along a metre of filament, average them, and put that in the measured diameter field. Doing the same in your slicer's filament profile improves its own estimates for the same reason.

When density matters

Most of the time a generic density is fine, but three cases bite. Filled filaments (carbon fiber, glass, wood, metal) are denser — metal-filled PLA can be 2.0–4.0 g/cm³, so a "1 kg" spool holds a fraction of the usual length. Foaming lightweight PLA is the opposite: about 1.2 g/cm³ on the spool but 0.5–0.6 after it expands in the hotend, so slicer weight estimates are only right if the profile's density matches what's on the reel. And flexible filaments vary between makers (1.15–1.25). If a spool's weight and the slicer's length disagree by more than a few percent, the density in the profile is the first suspect.
