The formula

new flow = current flow × line width set ÷ average measured wall

The slicer assumes each wall line comes out exactly as wide as the line width setting. If the wall is thicker than that, the printer is pushing too much plastic and the flow comes down in proportion; thinner, and it goes up. Because it's a ratio, it works whether your slicer shows flow as 100% or 1.00.

Worked example

Cube printed at 0.45 mm line width, 100% flow. Walls measure 0.48, 0.47, 0.49 and 0.48 mm.

  • Average: (0.48 + 0.47 + 0.49 + 0.48) ÷ 4 = 0.480 mm
  • New flow: 100% × 0.45 ÷ 0.480 = 93.8% — enter 94 in Cura, or 0.938 in Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer or PrusaSlicer

Printing the test cube

  1. Slice a 20 × 20 × 20 mm cube (any calibration cube model works, or add a plain cube in the slicer).
  2. Set 1 wall, 0 top layers, 0% infill. In Cura enable Spiralize Outer Contour; in PrusaSlicer/Orca/Bambu enable Spiral vase. Keep bottom layers — you want the base for stability.
  3. Use the same line width, layer height, temperature and speed you normally print with. Flow is partly a temperature effect, so calibrate the profile you'll actually use.
  4. Let it cool completely. Measure each wall with digital calipers about halfway up, squeezing gently — a plastic wall compresses. Take two readings per side if they disagree.
  5. Enter the four readings above. Apply the new flow, reprint, and expect the walls to land within ±0.02 mm of the set width.

Set it in the filament profile, not the print profile. Flow is a property of the material and the temperature, and different spools of the same material can differ by a few percent.

Order of operations

Flow interacts with everything upstream of it. Calibrate in this order and each step stays fixed:

  1. E-steps / rotation distance — once per extruder, so the right length of filament is delivered.
  2. Flow — this page, once per material.
  3. Temperature and retraction — with a test tower, per material.
  4. Dimensional accuracy — scale and hole compensation, per material.

How much to trust this number

Worth being straight about: the leading community reference disagrees with this method. Ellis' Print Tuning Guide lists "tuning extrusion multiplier by measuring 1–2 cube walls" as the first entry on its Misconceptions page. The reasoning is sound and worth understanding rather than ignoring:

  • The error ratio is brutal. A 0.02 mm slip with calipers — well within normal for squeezing a plastic wall — is about 4% of a 0.48 mm wall. That is larger than the correction most people are trying to make. The calculator now shows this figure live, from your own numbers, so you can see how much of the result is noise.
  • Measurements read high. A single unsupported wall bulges, and layer wobble and filament diameter variation both add width. The measurement captures all of that alongside the flow you are trying to isolate.
  • Small objects amplify everything. Dimensional work is more reliable on larger parts, where a fixed error is a smaller proportion.

None of that makes the arithmetic wrong — the formula is exact. It means the input carries more uncertainty than the output's decimal places suggest. Two runs landing within 1–2% of each other is as good as this method gets, and that is roughly the diameter tolerance of the filament itself.

What to do instead, or as well

  • Tune by top surface. Ellis' preferred approach: print test blocks at 1–2% flow increments and pick the one whose top surface is smoothest. Too much flow looks and feels rough; too little leaves gaps and valleys between lines. It is a visual judgement rather than a measurement, which is precisely why it sidesteps the caliper problem. Look at the widest flat areas of the print.
  • Use your slicer's built-in test. OrcaSlicer's Calibration → Flow Rate runs a two-pass version of the same visual method and applies the result for you.
  • Fix dimensions separately. If parts come out the wrong size, that is a job for shrinkage compensation and hole offsets, not for flow. Using flow to chase dimensional accuracy trades a good top surface for a number on the calipers.

Use this page when you want a fast numerical starting point, or to sanity-check a value you arrived at visually. If the two disagree, trust the top surface.
