The mistake this page exists to prevent

There are two numbers, they are reciprocals of each other, and different slicers want different ones. Entering the wrong one does not do nothing — it moves the part the wrong way and roughly doubles your error.

scale factor = designed ÷ measured → 100 ÷ 99.4 = 100.60%
measured percent = measured ÷ designed → 99.4 ÷ 100 = 99.40%

OrcaSlicer and Bambu Studio want the measured percentage. Their field asks what the material does, and the documentation is explicit: "Enter the shrinkage percentage that the filament will get after cooling (94% if you measure 94mm instead of 100mm). The part will be scaled in XY to compensate." You give it 99.4 and it does the rest.

PrusaSlicer, SuperSlicer and Cura want the scale factor, because none of them has a filament shrinkage field. You scale the model to 100.60% and they print it larger.

Pick your slicer above and the page gives you the one number for the one field. Both are shown lower down for reference.

Worked example

A 100 mm ASA square measures 99.4 mm in X and 99.5 mm in Y. Its 10 mm hole measures 9.6 mm.

  • XY average scale: 100 ÷ 99.45 = 100.55%
  • Measured percentage: 99.45 ÷ 100 = 99.45%
  • In OrcaSlicer, Shrinkage (XY) = 99.45
  • In PrusaSlicer, scale the object to 100.55%
  • Hole: (10 − 9.6) ÷ 2 = 0.2 mm per side of hole compensation

Reprint and measure again. Second-pass results are typically within 0.05 mm on a 100 mm part.

Shrinkage and hole error are different problems

It is worth being clear about why these need two separate corrections, because it explains why fixing one never fixes the other.

Shrinkage is proportional. The polymer contracts as it cools by a percentage of its size, so a 200 mm part loses twice as many millimetres as a 100 mm one. Two holes 100 mm apart end up 99.4 mm apart. The fix is a scale.

Hole error is roughly constant. The extruded bead bulges toward the centre on a concave curve, the slicer approximates a circle as a polygon that sits inside the true circle, and first-layer squish spreads inward. Those errors are measured in millimetres, not percentages — a 3 mm hole and a 30 mm hole both lose something like 0.2–0.4 mm of diameter. The fix is a fixed offset applied only to holes.

This is why you measure the hole after applying the scale. Otherwise the scale has already changed the hole a little and you correct the same error twice.

A note on PrusaSlicer

PrusaSlicer's XY size compensation is often suggested for holes, and it does work — but it offsets every contour in the layer, not just holes. A negative value large enough to open your holes up will shrink the outer dimensions by exactly the same amount per side. If only the holes matter, that is fine. If both matter on the same part, your options are to model the holes oversize in CAD, or use SuperSlicer or OrcaSlicer, which separate the two.

Printing a useful test part

Bigger is better, because you are measuring a percentage with an absolute instrument. A 20 mm cube shrinking 0.5% is 0.1 mm — inside caliper noise. A 100 mm part makes it 0.5 mm, which is unmistakable.

  • A 100 × 100 × 10 mm plate with a 10 mm and a 20 mm hole, or Orca's built-in tolerance test.
  • A 150 mm bar if the bed allows — longer is more precise.
  • For Z, a 50 mm tower measured with the calipers' depth rod rather than the jaws.

Print in the material and profile you will actually use, let it cool completely (ABS keeps moving for an hour), and measure each dimension at two points, mid-height, to avoid elephant's foot at the base.

When X and Y disagree

Material shrinkage is the same in both directions. If your X and Y corrections differ by more than about 0.15%, something mechanical is doing it — belt tension, a skewed frame, or a gantry that is not square. The calculator flags this, because compensating for it bakes the skew into every part you print instead of fixing the printer. The belt tension calculator is the place to start.
